SPECTR: The Model That Answers in Bits, Not Paragraphs

Most AI decisions need one bit. We pay for a paragraph. A hybrid architecture that picks its own output resolution closes the gap.

Bottom line up front

The claim. Today you can buy a decision two ways: a classifier that answers yes or no for a fraction of a cent, or a language model that writes you a paragraph for ten thousand times more. Nothing serves the middle. SPECTR (Spectrum Probabilistic Empirical Classification and Token Reduction) is a single model that outputs at four resolutions — 1 bit, 4 bits, 8 bits, or free text — and chooses the cheapest one that answers the question confidently.
Why it works. Decisions follow a steep distribution: the vast majority are routine and low-information, a thin tail needs explanation. A model that spends tokens only on the tail can cut cost per decision by roughly two orders of magnitude while keeping the full model in reserve.
The catch. The savings live or die on one number: how often the router escalates to full text. Every extra percentage point of escalation costs more than the entire low-resolution tier combined. Calibration is not a feature of SPECTR; it is the product.

Every figure below answers one question, and no two figures answer the same one. Read the plain reading if you want the idea; read the practitioner note if you plan to build it.

01 · Why: the resolution gap

Consider what an email filter, a fraud flag, a content-moderation gate, or an agent's "do I have enough context to act?" check actually need from a model. They need a decision. Frequently that decision is a single bit. Yet the tools on the shelf come in two sizes only: narrow classifiers built for one task, and general language models that express every answer as text.

The earlier model in this line of work, JEV, sits firmly at one end. It behaves like a System 1 reflex: fast, cheap, and structured, returning a choice, a score, or a noul — a hard true/false. The full language model sits at the other end. Between them lies a wide band of decisions that need more than a bit but far less than a paragraph — sixteen buckets, a priority score, a short code — and nothing is priced for them.

Figure 1 · Why — Two products exist; the middle of the market is empty

Plain reading. A bit costs about a penny's worth of a penny; a paragraph costs ten thousand times that. Almost everything you might want to ask sits in between, and nobody sells it.

For practitioners. Both axes are log. The staircase is not a claim of continuity — it is four discrete operating points sharing one trunk. Cost includes compute only; the routing overhead is treated in Figure 7.

02 · What: the resolution ladder

SPECTR's output is not "text or not text." It is a ladder with four rungs, each a strict superset of the one below. A bit answers a yes/no. A nibble (4 bits) picks one of sixteen buckets — enough to route an inbox, grade severity, or choose a tool. A byte (8 bits) gives 256 outcomes, or a coarse scalar such as a confidence score. Token output is reserved for the case where the answer needs to be explained, not merely given.

The word fractal in the original proposal is doing real work here: every rung is a lossy compression of the rung above. The bit is the sign of the byte; the nibble is its top half. That nesting is what makes one model able to serve all four without four separate training runs.

Figure 2 · What — Four output resolutions, each nested in the next

Plain reading. Think of a thermostat, a sixteen-button remote, a dial with 256 clicks, and a person who talks. Same building, four ways to answer, from cheapest to richest.

For practitioners. Levels are nested by construction: L1 = sign(L3), L2 = high nibble of L3. Token output need not be free-form — a constrained grammar (JSON, tool call) keeps L4 cheap and auditable while preserving richness.

03 · How it is built: one trunk, four heads, one router

Architecturally SPECTR is unremarkable on purpose. A shared encoder turns the input into a representation. A lightweight router reads that representation and predicts two things: which resolution is likely sufficient, and how confident it is. Four output heads sit on the trunk, one per rung. Only the chosen head runs. If the head's own confidence falls below threshold, control returns to the router, which steps up one rung.

What is unusual is the contract at the boundary. Every head, including the token head, emits a calibrated confidence alongside its answer. That single field is what makes the cascade safe to automate.

Figure 3 · How It Is Built — Shared trunk, adaptive router, four selectable heads

Plain reading. One brain reads the question once. A small dispatcher decides how much of an answer is needed and hands the job to the cheapest specialist who can do it. If that specialist hesitates, the job moves up a level.

For practitioners. The trunk is amortised across levels; heads L1–L3 are near-free linear layers on h. The router is a small MLP trained on (h → sufficient level) labels derived post hoc from head agreement. This is structurally an early-exit network with a learned exit policy, not a new primitive.

04 · How it runs: the cascade

At inference the model behaves like a triage nurse. Every request starts at the bit head. Most stop there. Those the bit head cannot resolve confidently step up to the nibble head, then the byte head, and only the residue reaches token generation. The shape of that funnel is the whole economic argument, so it deserves to be drawn rather than asserted.

Figure 4 · How It Runs — Where 100 requests stop in an illustrative cascade

Plain reading. Of a hundred questions, eighty are answered with a yes or no, fifteen by picking a bucket, four with a score, and one gets a written explanation. That one written answer costs almost everything.

For practitioners. The 80/15/4/1 split is a worked example, not a benchmark. Real splits are task-specific and shift under distribution drift, which is why the router must be monitored as a production metric (Figure 7). The 97% figure assumes relative per-level costs of 1 : 2 : 4 : 5,000.

05 · How it learns: multi-scale supervision

A naive way to build SPECTR is to train four models and bolt on a switch. That wastes the nesting. Instead, every training example carries targets at all four levels, and the lower-resolution targets are derived from the highest one: take the full answer, compress it to a byte-sized code, keep its top nibble, keep its sign. One forward pass, four losses, one weighted sum.

The effect is that the cheap heads learn to be faithful summaries of the expensive one rather than independent guessers. When the bit head says "block," it is predicting what the token head would have concluded, had it been asked.

Figure 5 · How It Learns — One example, four nested targets, one combined loss

Plain reading. You teach the model the full answer once. The short answers are just the full answer with detail removed, so the model learns all four at the same time and they agree with each other.

For practitioners. Target derivation requires a fixed codebook mapping text outcomes to 256 codes; learn it (VQ over trunk embeddings) or specify it (taxonomy). λ weights trade low-level accuracy against token fluency; anneal λ₁–λ₃ upward late in training so cheap heads sharpen after the trunk stabilises. The router loss uses labels of "lowest level whose head was correct."

06 · Why trust it: calibration at every rung

Cascades fail in one specific way: a cheap head that is confidently wrong. It never escalates, so the expensive head never gets the chance to catch the mistake. The defence is not a smarter cheap head; it is a cheap head that knows what it does not know. In other words, calibration — when the bit head says 90% confident, it should be right nine times in ten.

SPECTR treats calibration as a per-level property, measured and maintained separately, because the levels drift differently. A bit head stays honest easily; a token head, which is judging its own prose, is chronically overconfident.

Figure 6 · Why Trust It — Reliability at each level — how far stated confidence is from earned accuracy

Plain reading. Each square asks: when the model says it is 80% sure, is it right 80% of the time? Points on the dotted line mean yes. The talking level drifts below the line — it sounds more certain than it deserves.

For practitioners. Temperature-scale each head separately on a held-out set; re-fit on a schedule because drift is level-dependent. For the escalation threshold τ, prefer a conformal or selective-classification guarantee ("no more than 2% of L1 answers wrong") over a hand-tuned confidence cut-off. Monitor L1 false-accept rate as a top-line SLO.

07 · What it costs: the escalation rate is the price

Here is the sentence to remember. In a cascade, the blended cost per decision is almost entirely the share of requests that reach token generation, multiplied by the token cost. The low-resolution tiers are so cheap that they round to zero. That has two consequences: the upside is enormous, and it is fragile.

Figure 7 · What It Costs — Blended cost per decision as a function of token-escalation share

Plain reading. If one in a hundred questions needs a written answer, the whole system costs about a hundredth of always writing. If one in ten does, the saving shrinks to a tenth. The line is steep at the left: small changes in how often you escalate change the bill a lot.

For practitioners. d(cost)/d(p) ≈ ctok: each additional point of escalation costs roughly 50 units — more than the entire L1–L3 tier per decision. Treat escalation share as a budgeted, alerted production metric. Beware Goodhart: pressuring the router to cut escalation degrades L1 accuracy silently unless Figure 6's calibration guard is enforced. Router overhead (~0.5 units) is included in the 1.5 floor.

08 · Where it fits: two questions decide

SPECTR is not a universal replacement for either a classifier or a language model. It earns its place where two conditions hold together: decision volume is high enough that per-decision cost matters, and semantic demand is mixed — mostly routine, with a real tail that needs words. Ask those two questions of any workload and the answer falls into one of four boxes.

Figure 8 · Where It Fits — Workloads by decision volume and semantic demand

Plain reading. If almost nothing needs words, a plain yes/no model wins. If most things need words, use a language model. SPECTR is for the common in-between: lots of decisions, most simple, a few that need explaining.

For practitioners. Volume must be high enough that trunk amortisation and router overhead are recovered; below ~10⁵ decisions/day the engineering cost of a cascade rarely beats a single well-prompted LLM. Demand mixedness of 1–20% words is the band where Figure 7's curve is steepest and the saving most sensitive to router quality.

Part II — Where the classification space comes from

Bottom line up front · Part II

The gap in Part I. SPECTR assumes a codebook: two sides, sixteen buckets, up to 256 leaves. It never said who writes it or how it changes when the world does. A hand-written taxonomy is the weakest component in the design, because it is the one nobody retrains.
The fix. Borrow the machinery of EvoOntology (Chong, Zhang, Fan and Du, 2026), a self-evolving ontology layer built for data agents. Its typed term graph becomes SPECTR's partition tree, and its Diagnose → Attribute → Patch → Gate loop becomes the process by which SPECTR's own mistakes reshape that tree — under a validation gate that refuses any change that does not clear a margin.
Why it holds together. Depth in the ontology is resolution in SPECTR. The root split is the bit, the second tier is the nibble, the leaves are the byte, and "no leaf fits" is the signal to escalate to tokens. The 255-leaf ceiling is not arbitrary: it is 2⁸ − 1, the same per-question option cap Jev ships with today.

Six more figures, again one question each: what the ontology is, how the graph becomes the space, how the space changes, what moves are allowed, how regression is prevented, and how to build it.

09 · What the ontology is

EvoOntology solves a problem adjacent to ours. Data agents fail not because they cannot reason but because the meaning of the data is outside their reach: a column called cust_seg_v3 means nothing until someone says so. The paper's answer is an ontology the agent can query through tools, built automatically from the workload and refined from the agent's own failures. The state at any moment is a triple ℒₜ = (𝒮ₜ, Γₜ, ℛₜ): a content layer, a schema layer, and a tool layer.

The content layer is the part SPECTR cares about. It is a typed property graph with four kinds of node — Terms, Mappings to concrete fields, Constraints, and Evidence records — and two kinds of edge: semantic relations (hierarchy, composition, equivalence, association, derivation) and structural references. Every edit to the ontology is typed by which layer it touches, and the paper's ablation shows why that matters: tool edits alone gave +13.2 points on its main benchmark, content edits +8.7, schema edits +3.6, and all three together +20.0. The levels are complementary, and keeping them separate is what makes a regression traceable.

Figure 9 · What The Ontology Is — EvoOntology's three layers and the edits that touch each

Plain reading. An ontology is a shared dictionary of what the data means. EvoOntology keeps three things: the dictionary itself, the rules for what may go in it, and the buttons an AI agent presses to consult it. Each can be edited separately.

For practitioners. State is ℒₜ = (𝒮ₜ, Γₜ, ℛₜ). The property graph has four node families and two edge families; hierarchy edges are what SPECTR will read as its partition tree. The edit attribution α: Σ → {C, T, S} is the paper's key discipline — one level per patch, so a regression is diagnosable.

10 · How the graph becomes the space

Here is the load-bearing idea. Read the hierarchy edges of the content layer as a tree. The root's two children are the bit. The terms at depth two are the nibble — padded to sixteen slots, with unused slots left empty rather than filled with invented categories. The leaves are the byte, with code zero reserved for "this input belongs to no leaf." Anything that lands on code zero is, by definition, outside what the structured heads can express, and that is exactly the case the token head exists for.

This mapping is what makes the multi-scale training of Figure 5 free. Because codes are assigned depth-first, a leaf's byte contains its nibble as the high four bits and its root side as the top bit. The compression targets are not designed; they are read off the tree.

Figure 10 · How The Graph Becomes The Space — Depth in the term hierarchy is resolution in SPECTR

Plain reading. Picture the ontology as a family tree of categories. The top branch is the yes/no. The next row of sixteen is the "which bucket". The leaves are the fine labels. Anything the tree has no leaf for gets sent to the model that can write.

For practitioners. Compile 𝒮ₜ's hierarchy edges into a nested codebook: L1 = argmax over depth-1 children, L2 = depth-2 index (pad unused slots, never fill them), L3 = DFS leaf order with 0 reserved for out-of-ontology. The nesting L1 = top bit, L2 = high nibble of L3 is what lets Figure 5's compression targets fall out for free. Depth-2 fan-out above 16 forces a schema edit (Figure 12), not a truncation.

11 · How the space changes

EvoOntology's second phase is a loop. Diagnose recurrent failure signatures from the agent's trajectories. Attribute each signature to one level — content, schema, or tool. Patch with a single localised edit that tests one hypothesis. Gate the candidate against the parent on a held-out set, accepting only if it wins by a margin τ. Then repeat. The paper reports that removing the gate costs 11.2 points and removing attribution costs 6.3; neither step is decoration.

For SPECTR the trajectories are inference logs: which level answered, with what confidence, and whether it was right. The failure signatures are the ones Part I already told you to monitor — L1 false-accepts, code-zero rate, escalation spikes, persistent confusion between two leaves. Attribution maps content edits to the codebook, schema edits to the tree's shape, and tool edits to the heads and router.

Figure 11 · How The Space Changes — EvoOntology's four-step loop, driven by SPECTR's own trajectories

Plain reading. Watch where the model keeps getting things wrong, work out whether the fault is a missing category, a badly shaped tree, or a weak head, make one small fix, and keep it only if it measurably helps on a test set it hasn't seen.

For practitioners. Swap the paper's data-agent trajectories for SPECTR inference logs. Attribution maps C→Codebook (leaf set), S→Tree (hierarchy/fan-out), T→Heads/Router. φ must be the composite the business cares about — L1–L3 accuracy, ECE penalty, escalation share — evaluated for candidate and parent under identical budgets. Set τ above validation noise or you accept drift.

12 · What moves are allowed

An evolving classification space sounds dangerous, and it would be if any edit were allowed. The discipline is that only five typed moves exist, and each has a known blast radius. Splitting, merging, or retiring a leaf touches only the byte head. Re-parenting a subtree changes a nibble assignment and so retrains two heads. Re-splitting the root rewrites every nested target and should be treated as a new model version with a human in the loop. The gate's margin scales with that blast radius.

Figure 12 · What Moves Are Allowed — Five typed edits and which SPECTR heads each one forces to retrain

Plain reading. The category tree can only change in five ways, from cheap and local (splitting one label in two) to rare and drastic (changing the yes/no question at the top). The bigger the change, the more of the model has to relearn, and the higher the bar it must clear.

For practitioners. Edits are typed so blast radius is known in advance: leaf edits invalidate only L3 rows (fine-tune the byte head on the new codebook, freeze the trunk); re-parenting changes a nibble assignment and re-derives L2 targets; a root re-split rewrites every nested target and should be treated as a new model version. Scale τ with blast radius, and reserve a block of byte codes so splits never renumber existing leaves.

13 · How regression is prevented

The gate is a paired comparison: candidate and parent are scored on the same validation set under identical decoding and interaction budgets, and the candidate is kept only if φ(ℒ′ₜ) − φ(ℒₜ) ≥ τ. A small positive difference is rejected, because it is indistinguishable from noise. For SPECTR, φ must be the composite the business actually cares about: accuracy at L1–L3, an explicit penalty on calibration error, and a penalty on escalation share above budget. If φ is accuracy alone, the loop will happily buy accuracy by escalating everything, and Figure 7 tells you what that costs.

Figure 13 · How Regression Is Prevented — Paired validation with a margin, and what convergence looks like

Plain reading. Every proposed change is tested against the current version on the same exam. It has to win by a clear margin, not just squeak ahead, or it is thrown out. Over a handful of rounds the score climbs and then levels off — the tree has learned what the traffic needs.

For practitioners. ℒₜ₊₁ = ℒ′ₜ iff φ(ℒ′ₜ, 𝒱; m) − φ(ℒₜ, 𝒱; m) ≥ τ, conditioned on the backbone m. Use a frozen 𝒱 with periodic refresh to avoid overfitting the gate; bootstrap Δφ to set τ at, say, the 95th percentile of null differences. Convergence flattening is your signal to slow the loop, not to stop logging.

14 · How to build it

The paper's two phases map directly onto a build sequence. Phase one is evidence-grounded initialisation: propose candidate concepts from the historical workload, probe each against the data, verify, and commit only what survives. Phase two is the trajectory-grounded loop running against production logs. Seven components are needed, and the order that de-risks fastest is not the order the diagram reads: build the codebook compiler and the SPECTR trainer first against a hand-written tree, then the trajectory store and the gate, then the diagnose-attribute-patch loop, and only last the builder agent that proposes concepts on its own. A hand-written tree with a gate in front of it already proves the economics; the agent is an accelerant, not a prerequisite.

Figure 14 · How To Build It — Two phases, seven components, and what flows between them

Plain reading. First, build the category tree from the decisions you already have, checking each category against real data. Train the model on it and ship. Then let the model's own mistakes propose small changes to the tree, and keep only the ones that pass the test.

For practitioners. Phase 1 is the paper's workload-guided probing: propose concepts from 𝒲, probe them against the data, commit only verified ones. Phase 2 is its trajectory loop with SPECTR logs as the trajectory source. Build order that de-risks fastest: 3 → 4 → 5 → 7 → 6 → 1 → 2 — a hand-written tree with the compiler, trainer, store and gate in place proves the economics before any agent proposes anything.

What Part II changes about Part I

Three things. First, the codebook is no longer an input to SPECTR; it is an output of a loop that SPECTR feeds. Second, the escalation share of Figure 7 acquires a new cause and a new cure: a rising code-zero rate means the tree has stopped covering the traffic, and the cure is a content edit, not a bigger token budget. Third, the risk table in Part I gains a row — an ontology that evolves toward the validation set — and the paper's own answer is the one to copy: freeze 𝒱, refresh it on a schedule, and set τ above the noise floor rather than at zero.

What this is not

Practitioners will recognise the ancestry, and it is better to name it than to pretend novelty. Early-exit networks and adaptive computation gave us the idea that a model can stop when confident. Model cascades and routing frameworks gave us the idea of sending easy requests to cheap models first. Selective classification and conformal prediction gave us principled ways to decide when to abstain and pass the question along. Quantised and discrete latent representations gave us the tools to define a byte-sized codebook over meanings.

SPECTR's contribution is not any one of those. It is the insistence that the four resolutions be nested outputs of one model trained together, so the cheap answer is a faithful compression of the expensive one rather than a different opinion, and that escalation share be treated as the governing economic variable rather than a tuning detail.

Where the risk lives

How you would build it

Start with what already exists. Take a trunk you trust and add bit and nibble heads with a hand-written threshold; measure the escalation share on real traffic before touching anything else. That number alone tells you whether the economics are there. If they are, add the byte head and the learned router, then replace the hand threshold with a conformal one. Only then invest in full multi-scale training. Each step is independently useful and independently reversible.

The cheapest answer is not the shortest one. It is the shortest one that is still right — and that knows when it might not be.
